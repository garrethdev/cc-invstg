# Bank for International Settlements (BIS): the founders

Compiled 26 Sep 2026. Sources: BIS official history (bis.org/about/history), Gianni Toniolo, *Central Bank Cooperation at the BIS 1930-1973* (CUP, as cited on Wikipedia), Frank Costigliola, "The Other Side of Isolationism: The Establishment of the First World Bank, 1929-1930" (J. of American History, 1972), Beth Simmons, "Why Innovate? Founding the BIS" (World Politics, 1993), Adam LeBor, *Tower of Basel* (2013), Wikipedia (accessed today).

## 1. Timeline of the founding

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| Sep 1925 | Montagu Norman (Bank of England) writes of wanting "a private and eclectic Central Banks' Club" |
| Jul 1927 | Norman, Benjamin Strong (NY Fed), Hjalmar Schacht (Reichsbank), Charles Rist (Bank of France) meet privately on Long Island |
| 9 Feb - 7 Jun 1929 | Young Committee on German reparations sits in Paris (Bank of France, then Hotel George V) |
| 23 Feb 1929 | Emile Francqui (Belgium) presents first draft concept of a settlements bank; Schacht follows with his own proposal and coins the name "International Settlements Bank" in a memo to Young |
| 7 Mar 1929 | Young's US aides Burgess, Shepard Morgan and Walter W. Stewart present the compromise text that became the basis of the bank |
| 25 Mar 1929 | Young Committee agrees the bank; draft statutes written by Sir Charles Addis (Bank of England) |
| Aug 1929 | First Hague Conference accepts the Young Plan and the BIS concept |
| 3 Oct - 13 Nov 1929 | Organisation Committee at Hotel Stephanie, Baden-Baden, chaired by Jackson Reynolds, drafts Charter, Statutes, Trust Agreement and host-country Convention. Basel chosen over London, Brussels and Amsterdam. Leon Delacroix dies of a heart attack during the meetings |
| 20 Jan 1930 | Second Hague Conference adopts the Young Plan and signs the BIS founding texts and the Convention with Switzerland |
| 26 Feb 1930 | Swiss Federal Council grants the Charter |
| 27 Feb 1930 | Governors sign the Statutes in Rome (meeting chaired by Bonaldo Stringher). Legal birth of the BIS |
| 22-23 Apr 1930 | First informal Board meeting in Basel; officers elected |
| 17 May 1930 | BIS opens for business at the former Hotel Savoy-Univers, Centralbahnstrasse 7, Basel; first formal Board meeting |
| 20 May 1930 | Shares publicly issued |

## 2. Founding governments and central banks

Convention signatories: Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom (and, per the Wikipedia account, the United States as a Young Plan party) on one side, and Switzerland as host on the other.

Founding subscriber central banks (each governor an ex officio director with the right to name a second director; France and Germany a third during the Young Plan):
- National Bank of Belgium (Louis Franck)
- Bank of France (Emile Moreau)
- Reichsbank (Hjalmar Schacht; Hans Luther from March 1930)
- Bank of Italy (Bonaldo Stringher)
- Bank of Japan (represented via its London office; shares held by a Japanese bank group)
- Bank of England (Montagu Norman)
- United States: the Federal Reserve was barred by the Hoover administration from taking part, so the US tranche was subscribed by a private group of three banks: J.P. Morgan & Co., First National Bank of New York and First National Bank of Chicago.

The US, Belgian and French tranches were wholly or partly sold on to private investors, which is why the BIS had private shareholders and stock-exchange-listed shares until the 2001-2005 buyout (contested by First Eagle; final price SFr 25,052.90 per share).

## 3. The individual founders

### Conceptual precursors (before 1929)
- Julius Wolff (Germany), 1892 Brussels conference: blueprint for an emergency-lending international currency issued from a neutral country.
- Raphael-Georges Levy (France), 1893: proposed an international central bank in Bern.
- Luigi Luzzatti (Italy), 1907: article in *Neue Freie Presse* calling for institutionalised central bank cooperation.
- Gerard Vissering (Netherlands), Frank A. Vanderlip (US), Leon Delacroix (Belgium): 1920 Brussels conference ideas.
- Ralph Hawtrey, Robert Horne, J.M. Keynes: 1922 Genoa resolution for "an association or permanent understanding for cooperation amongst central banks".
- Montagu Norman and Benjamin Strong: the 1925-27 "central bankers' club" idea. Strong died Oct 1928 before the bank existed.

### The Americans (Costigliola's "first world bank" group)
- Owen D. Young (1874-1962): GE and RCA chairman, Democratic foreign-policy voice, chair of the Young Committee. Central figure in conceiving and pushing the bank through. Saw it as economic integration leading to political integration and the survival of capitalism.
- J.P. Morgan Jr. (1867-1943): Young Committee delegate, monitored Baden-Baden from London, ensured Americans held the top posts at launch. J.P. Morgan & Co. was a founding US shareholder.
- Thomas W. Lamont (1870-1948): Morgan partner, Young Committee alternate and negotiator.
- S. Parker Gilbert (1892-1938): Agent General for Reparation Payments 1924-30, whose office the BIS absorbed; later Morgan partner.
- Jackson E. Reynolds (1873-1958): president, First National Bank of New York; chaired the Baden-Baden Organisation Committee that wrote the Charter and Statutes.
- Melvin A. Traylor (1878-1934): president, First National Bank of Chicago; Baden-Baden delegate. With Reynolds he pushed McGarrah for the presidency over Bank of England objections.
- Gates W. McGarrah (1863-1940): chairman of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York; resigned to become the first BIS President and Chairman of the Board (1930-33).
- Leon Fraser (1889-1945): lawyer with the Agent General's office; first alternate President, then President 1933-35; one of the two US directors on the first Board. Later president of First National Bank of New York.
- W. Randolph Burgess, Shepard Morgan, Walter W. Stewart: Young's technical drafting team (7 March 1929 text; Burgess and Morgan also at Baden-Baden).

### The Europeans and Japanese
- Hjalmar Schacht (Germany, 1877-1970): Reichsbank president; put forward the settlements-bank proposal the day after Francqui, named it, and argued for a development-lending role that France and the commercial bankers blocked. Resigned from the Reichsbank March 1930 in protest at the Young Plan, so did not sit on the first Board; returned as Reichsbank president under Hitler 1933-39 and was a BIS director again. Acquitted at Nuremberg.
- Montagu Norman (UK, 1871-1950): Bank of England governor 1920-44; the intellectual father of the "central bankers' club"; wanted a bank purely of central banks. Board member throughout the 1930s; approved the 1939 Czech gold transfer.
- Sir Charles Addis (UK, 1861-1945): Bank of England director, ex-HSBC; wrote the draft Statutes; first Vice-Chairman.
- Emile Moreau (France, 1868-1950): Bank of France governor; amended Francqui's draft; first Vice-Chairman.
- Pierre Quesnay (France, 1895-1937): Bank of France economist; first General Manager (Apr 1930-Sep 1937), elected over the three German directors' votes.
- Charles Rist (France): Bank of France deputy governor, Long Island 1927 participant.
- Emile Francqui (Belgium, 1863-1935): Societe Generale de Belgique; author of the first draft concept (23 Feb 1929); first Board member.
- Louis Franck (Belgium, 1868-1937): National Bank of Belgium governor; first Board member.
- Leon Delacroix (Belgium, 1867-1929): former prime minister, early promoter; died during the Baden-Baden drafting.
- Paul van Zeeland (Belgium): Rome signatory, later prime minister.
- Bonaldo Stringher (Italy, 1854-1930): Bank of Italy governor; chaired the Rome founding meeting; died Dec 1930.
- Alberto Beneduce (Italy, 1877-1944): IRI founder; Young Committee delegate; first Board member; with Norman argued for a central-banks-only institution.
- Vincenzo Azzolini (Italy): Rome signatory, later Bank of Italy governor.
- Hans Luther (Germany, 1879-1962): ex-Chancellor, Reichsbank president from Mar 1930; first Board member.
- Carl Melchior (Germany, 1871-1933): M.M. Warburg partner; Young Committee delegate; first Vice-Chairman.
- Paul Reusch (Germany): Gutehoffnungshutte industrialist; first Board member.
- Tetsuzaburo Tanaka and Daisuke Nohara (Japan): Bank of Japan London representatives; first Board members. Hiroshi Saito signed at Rome.
- Per Jacobsson (Sweden, 1894-1963): first Economic Adviser (1931), built the research and Annual Report; later IMF Managing Director.

## 4. The first Board (16 members, 1930)
Belgium: Franck, Francqui. France: Moreau, Georges Brincard, Melchior de Vogue. Germany: Luther, Melchior, Reusch. Italy: Stringher, Beneduce. Japan: Tanaka, Nohara. UK: Norman, Addis. US: McGarrah, Fraser.

First officers (elected 22-23 Apr 1930, ratified 17 May): McGarrah President and Chairman; Fraser alternate President; Addis, Melchior, Moreau Vice-Chairmen of equal rank; Quesnay General Manager.

## 5. Why they built it (motives by party)
- Reparations mechanics: replace the Agent General; receive, manage and distribute German annuities; commercialise part of them via bonds; monitor Germany and propose revisions on default (Simmons).
- US bankers and the NY Fed: extend the "federal principle" of central bank cooperation abroad, cement New York's ascendancy in world banking, and rebuild a Europe that could absorb US exports, while Washington stayed officially aloof (Costigliola).
- Britain: a cooperative mechanism to defend a thinly reserved pound; Norman's club.
- France: enforce payment; keep the bank from becoming a competitor to private lending or an inflationary development bank.
- Germany (Schacht): access to long-term international credit, and the possibility of a "world bank" for trade.
- Structural design: a Swiss limited company with private-law shares but de facto international personality, tax and supervisory immunity, diplomatic status for management, and a charter immunising its assets from seizure in peace or war.

## 6. Things worth knowing when reading conspiracy-flavoured material about the founders
- The Fed was not a founder and did not join the Board until 1994. The American seats came via private banks.
- The BIS moved out of the old Savoy-Univers hotel opposite Basel SBB in 1977 into the round Martin Burckhardt tower; the 1998 Botta building houses banking operations.
- BIS gold held for central banks sits mostly at custodian central banks, not in a vault under Basel.
- The genuinely documented dark chapters are 1939-45 (Czech gold transfer, looted Reichsbank gold, Nazi directors Funk, Schmitz, von Schroeder; Puhl), not the 1929-30 founding group.
