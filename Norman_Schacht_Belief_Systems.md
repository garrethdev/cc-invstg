# Montagu Norman and Hjalmar Schacht: belief systems, with archival quotations

Compiled 26 Sep 2026. Grading: (a) verbatim, solid primary or archival source; (b) second-hand or recollected; (c) unverified or fabricated.
Main sources: Bank of England Archive (Norman diaries ADM34; Governor's files G1/415, G1/419; unpublished War History pp. 1293-96; Sayers interview files ADM/33); UK National Archives T 160/197, T 172/1499; Macmillan Committee minutes 1930; Kynaston, Till Time's Last Sand (2017); Morrison, England's Cross of Gold (2021); Sayers, The Bank of England 1891-1944 (1976); Boyle, Montagu Norman (1967); Toniolo, Central Bank Cooperation at the BIS (2005); Ahamed, Lords of Finance (2009); LeBor, Tower of Basel (2013); Nuremberg IMT transcripts 30 Apr-3 May 1946 and Judgment (Avalon); Nazi Conspiracy and Aggression vol. 2 ch. XVI; Bundesarchiv R 43 II/234 (7 Jan 1939 memo); Schacht, The Magic of Money (1967), Confessions of the Old Wizard (1956), 76 Jahre meines Lebens (1953); Goldensohn, The Nuremberg Interviews (2004).

## PART I. MONTAGU NORMAN (1871-1950), Governor of the Bank of England 1920-44

### Core creed in one line
Money is too important to be left to politicians. Central banks must be autonomous, must cooperate with each other across borders, and must be anchored to gold. Everything else, including democracy's claims on credit policy, is subordinate.

### 1. Central bank independence and secrecy
- (a) His 1921 "manifesto" of central banking principles, circulated to Benjamin Strong and others: "1. Autonomy and freedom from political control are desirable for all Central and Reserve Banks. 2. Subject to conformity with the above clause a policy of continuous cooperation is desirable among Central and Reserve Banks." [Sayers 1976, Appendixes p. 75]
- (b) Personal motto: "Never explain, never excuse." [Boyle 1967 p. 217; Bernanke 2007 says "reputedly"]
- (b) To Henry Clay, the Bank's first economist, 1933: "You are not here to tell us what to do, but to explain to us why we have done it." [Clay's recollection, via Kynaston]
- (a, partial) Evidence 1926: "I look upon the Bank as having the unique right to offer advice and to press such advice even to the point of nagging; but always of course subject to the supreme authority of the Government." [Sayers vol. 1; second half needs checking against original]
- (a) Norman's memo of his Sept 1929 concordat with Chancellor Snowden: "The Governor said that his was the technical and financial side - the Chancellor's was the political and fiscal side. On this basis the Chancellor must now leave the Bank rate to the Governor." [BoE archive, via Kynaston]
- (a) To Treasury's Hopkins, autumn 1929, resisting the Macmillan inquiry: "the mere promise of any such Committee under present conditions would of itself endanger our financial position, both at home and abroad. Indeed, the maintenance of the gold standard itself might by such action be suddenly and dangerously threatened." [Kynaston]
- (b) He "often said": "the Treasury is your worst enemy. Inflation always comes from the governments." [F. Conolly interview 1970, BoE ADM/33/26]
- (b) Aug 1931, asked if Britain would pull through: "Yes, if we can get them frightened enough" (the Labour government). [Kynaston]
- Contemporaries: Reginald McKenna to the Macmillan Committee in private, 1930: the Governor exercised "a mute and irresponsible despotism". Keynes: "always absolutely charming, always absolutely wrong." Asked how he knew something, Norman "simply replied by tapping his nose three times."

### 2. Gold and sound money
- (a) Commentary for Churchill, 2 Feb 1925, on returning to gold: "The Chancellor will surely be charged with a sin of omission or of commission. In the former case (Gold) he will be abused by the ignorant, the gamblers and the antiquated Industrialists; in the latter case (not Gold) he will be abused by the instructed and by posterity." [NA T 172/1499B, via Morrison]
- (a) Same document: "The restoration of Free Gold will require a high Bank rate."
- (a) Chamberlain-Bradbury Committee, 27 June 1924: "It is much more a question of the psychology of the announcement than of the facts." [NA T 160/197/6]
- (a) Macmillan Committee, March 1930: "I think that the disadvantages to the internal position are relatively small compared with the advantages to the external position." And: "the whole international position has preserved for us in this country the wonderful position which we have inherited ... We are still to a large extent international bankers ... a free gold market, a free exchange market - perhaps the freest almost in the world." To Ernest Bevin on the 1925 parity: "I do not attribute the ills of industry in the main to that change." [Minutes of Evidence, via Kynaston]
- (a/b) Letter to Clement Moret of the Banque de France, spring 1931, leaked: "Unless drastic measures are taken to save it, the capitalist system throughout the civilized world will be wrecked within a year. I should like this prediction to be filed for future reference." [Ahamed; Quincy Howe, World Diary p. 111]
- (a) Diary, 10 June 1931: "I say Germany is a good bet in the long run & needs help & comfort rather than worrying." [BoE ADM34]
- (a) Diary, 29 July 1931, the day of his breakdown: "12.30 about - left C. Treasy and went home about 3 o'clock. Queer."
- (c) "The gold standard is knave-proof" is Bradbury's phrase to Churchill, not Norman's. "I don't have reasons, I have instincts" is apocryphal.
- Contemporaries: Churchill, Aug 1931: "I hope we shall hang Montagu Norman if it does. I will certainly turn King's evidence against him." Churchill in 1945: the return to gold was "the biggest blunder in his life ... Montagu Norman had spread his blandishments before him till it was done, and had then left him severely alone."

### 3. The central bankers' club and the BIS
- (a) Sept 1925: "I rather hope that next summer we may be able to inaugurate a private and eclectic Central Banks' 'Club', small at first, large in the future." [Toniolo 2005 p. 30 gives addressee as Strong; Kynaston says Siepmann. Kynaston continues the passage: subscriptions "in the shape of exclusive relations; appropriate balances with other Central Banks; ... no undue regard for profit; political freedom by right or by custom; credits when there is bad weather in any particular place."]
- (a) To Schacht, May 1925: the object of central bank cooperation was "to secure regulation of rates of interest and exchange - and so prices of gold and commodities - and by these means the improvement of international trade". [Kynaston]
- (a) From the Hotel Adlon, Berlin, July 1925, on Strong and Schacht: "He & Schacht are making friends & a good feeling is growing up from which much else may follow, sooner or later." [Kynaston]
- (b) On the BIS's purpose: "to make an umbrella, so that we can all get under it when it rains." [R.N. Kershaw interview 1969, BoE ADM/33/25]
- (b) To Walter Layton, 1929, while drafting the BIS statutes: find "some form of words that would place the bank beyond the reach of governments." Layton objected that democratic governments must keep their freedom of action. Norman: "Why do you insist it can't be done?" [Boyle p. 247]
- (a) To Snowden, 1929: the BIS was "the only way for Europe out of financial chaos" and would let central bankers meet on "neutral soil". [Kynaston]
- (a) To Peter Bark, Nov 1930: "The longer I go on, the harder do I find the path of an internationalist!" [Kynaston]
- Contemporaries: Strong, 1928: it was "current in Europe ... that Governor Norman desired to establish some sort of dictatorship over the central banks of Europe". Emile Moreau to Poincare, 1928, on "the Bank of England's imperialism": "England has managed to install itself completely in Austria, Hungary, Belgium, Norway and Italy."
- (c) FABRICATED: the widely shared "1924" quotation ("Capital must protect itself in every way ... The hegemony of world finance should reign supreme over everyone, everywhere, as one whole super-national control mechanism"). No letter, speech, archive file or newspaper. Originates as an anonymous "bankers' circular" in 1920s-30s US populist papers, later grafted onto Norman. Absent from every scholarly biography. Also unsourced: "currency dictator of Europe ... admitted before the Court 21 March 1930" (only in Quigley, Tragedy and Hope p. 62).

### 4. Germany, Schacht, appeasement, the Czech gold
- (a) BoE Governor's file G1/419, "Dr Hjalmar Schacht - Governor Norman's visit to Berlin", 28 Aug 1938 - 7 Feb 1939: "Norman to be the godfather to Schacht's grandson and to attend christening, 5 January 1939." The boy was named Norman. Schacht was dismissed by Hitler fourteen days later.
- (b) Via US Treasury (Morgenthau Diaries, Bk 164 p. 324; Bk 166 p. 104), Feb 1939: Norman called Schacht his main source on Germany "for the last sixteen years" and said his firing raised concerns "over the question of business morality ... If the capitalist system is to survive, that must be improved."
- (a) After the March 1939 transfer of Czechoslovak gold to the Reichsbank via the BIS: "I can't imagine any step more improper than to bring government into the current banking affairs of the BIS. I guess it would mean ruin. I imagine the Germans would never have paid any interest to the BIS, and at the board we would have then likely have found the Germans, Italians, and Japs standing together!" [Toniolo 2005 p. 209; LeBor]
- (a) To the Treasury, 22 March 1939, refusing a joint protest with the Banque de France: "it would be wrong and dangerous ... to attempt for political reasons to influence the decisions of the president of the BIS." [BoE War History p. 1294]
- (a) BoE War History (1950) on his reply to Chancellor Simon, 30 May 1939: "The Governor in his reply did not answer the question, but pointed out that the Bank held gold from time to time for the BIS and had no knowledge whether it was their own property or that of their customers."
- (a) To Hopkins, 4 Sept 1939, the day after war was declared, on breaking BIS rules: it "would offer hostile propaganda an excellent opportunity for criticism that, where it is in their interest, HM Government do not hesitate to disregard their international arrangements". [BoE War History p. 1295]
- (a) On Mussolini's Italy, 1927: "opposition in any form is gone: freedom of speech, opinion, criticism and press - even private life as we understand it"; yet "the fact remains that she has made economic and financial progress and is probably making social progress too". [Kynaston]
- (b) Hearsay via Ahamed: he "is said to have told a Morgan partner" that "Hitler and Schacht are the bulwarks of civilisation in Germany". The longer version adding "and the only friends we have" has no source.

### 5. Industry, the City, the public
- (a) Nov 1928: "the great industries can only be satisfactorily re-established by means of rationalisation." [Kynaston]
- (a) To the Court, Nov 1929: restore industry "by private enterprise and without government intervention."
- (a) On the Lancashire Cotton Corporation he had sponsored: an "association of lame ducks".
- (a) 1930, on Niemeyer's Australian mission: "I hope our good Niemeyer will succeed in converting these remote Australians to economic sanity and indeed to reason, but they have a long way to go."
- (a) To Strong, 1927, on the Financial Times: "A second class sheet which prides itself on irritation and sore spots and tail twisting."
- Contemporaries: Evening Standard 1929: "out of touch with the flesh and bones of industry". Earl of Crawford 1930: "they are usurers and nothing else ... The banks sail serenely above the tempests of industrial trouble."

### 6. Temperament and mysticism
- Consulted Carl Jung in April 1913 after a breakdown [Boyle; Mirwis, American Imago 1987]. Travelled incognito as "Professor Skinner" (the name of his private secretary Ernest Skinner) [Kynaston; Hargrave 1939 title]. Took cures at Baden-Baden. Second breakdown July-Aug 1931; press statement 15 Aug 1931: "indisposed as a result of the exceptional strain".
- (c) A documented interest in Theosophy was not confirmed in any source reached; do not assert without a Boyle page reference.
- Contemporaries: Jean Monnet: "Montagu Norman was the man who governed the citadel. He was redoubtable." Thomas Lamont: "the wisest man he had ever met". Snowden: he "might have stepped out of the frame of the portrait of the most handsome courtier who ever graced the court of a queen."

## PART II. HJALMAR SCHACHT (1877-1970), Reichsbank President 1923-30 and 1933-39, Economics Minister 1934-37

### Core creed in one line
A nationalist technician: sound money and a balanced budget are sacred, but they serve the restoration of German power. He believed he could ride Hitler to reverse Versailles and rearm, then rein him in. He never accepted that the second half was impossible.

### 1. Sound money and the "Old Wizard"
- (a) The Magic of Money (1967) ch. 1: "nothing in the world has so little to do with magic as dealing with money. Money must be handled with clear and cool calculation ... Money can no more be conjured into existence than the alchemists were able to make gold."
- (a) Same: "the politics of currency and money is not an exact science, but applied art."
- (a) On becoming Currency Commissioner, Nov 1923: "I gave up my highly-paid post as principal of one of the four largest German banks, I resigned from over seventy lucrative positions on supervisory boards, all because an ideal drew me on."
- (a) Reichsbank Directorate memorandum to Hitler, 7 Jan 1939 [Bundesarchiv R 43 II/234; IMT EC-369]: "Unrestrained public expenditure constitutes a definite threat to our currency. The unlimited growth of government expenditure defies any attempts to draw up a regulated budget ... No bank of issue is capable of maintaining the currency against an inflationary spending policy of the State." And: "An increase in the production of goods is not possible through an increase in paper money." And: "No gold or foreign-exchange reserves remain at the Reichsbank." Hitler's reaction, per Schacht: "This is mutiny." Note: Wilhelm Vocke later claimed authorship of the draft; Schacht signed first. The memo also boasts of having financed rearmament "out of nothing and, at first, under camouflage".
- (a) May 1936 secret ministerial minutes (1301-PS): he would "never be party to an inflation".
- (c) "The first cause of stability of our currency is the concentration camp" is Hitler speaking to Schacht, not Schacht.

### 2. Nationalism, Versailles, colonies, living space
- (a) 1927: "The Versailles Dictate cannot be an eternal document, because not only its economic, but also its spiritual and moral premises are wrong." [EC-415]
- (a) Young Plan conference, Paris, 16 Apr 1929: "Germany can generally only pay if the corridor and Upper Silesia will be handed back to Germany from Polish possession, and if besides somewhere on the earth colonial territory will be made available to Germany." [3726-PS]
- (a) Das Ende der Reparationen (1931) p. 243: "A capitalism that is not able to feed the working masses of the world has no right to exist."
- (a) Jan 1936: "The spirit of Versailles has perpetuated the fury of war, and there will not be a true peace, progress or reconstruction until the world desists from this spirit." [EC-415]
- (a) Frankfurt, 9 Dec 1936: "Germany has too little living space for her population ... in spite of all these efforts the space does not suffice." [EC-415]
- (b) To S.R. Fuller at the US Embassy, 23 Sept 1935: "Colonies are necessary to Germany. We shall get them through negotiation if possible; but if not, we shall take them." [EC-450]. Schacht disputed only "take" vs "get". Versions adding "by force of arms" are prosecution paraphrase.
- (a) Nuremberg, 30 Apr 1946: "I had a keen interest in diverting Hitler's quite misguided ideas from Eastern Europe and therefore was constantly at pains to direct his attention to the colonial problem."
- (a) On rearmament as creed: "I have always considered a rearmament of the German people as condition sine qua non of the establishment of a new German nation." [EC-257]

### 3. Hitler and National Socialism
- (a) Letter to Hitler, 29 Aug 1932 [EC-457]: "Your movement is carried internally by so strong a truth and necessity that victory in one form or another cannot elude you for long ... you can always count on me as your reliable assistant."
- (a) Letter to Hitler, 12 Nov 1932 [EC-456]: "I have no doubt that the present development of things can only lead to your becoming chancellor ... the present system is certainly doomed to disintegration." He drafted the industrialists' petition to Hindenburg of 19 Nov 1932 asking that a presidential cabinet be handed to "the leader of the largest national group".
- (b) Goebbels diary, 21 Nov 1932: "Dr. Schacht ... absolutely represents our point of view. He is one of the few who accepts the Fuehrer's position entirely."
- (a) Leipzig, 4 Mar 1935 [EC-503]: "I assure you that all that I am doing and saying enjoys the absolute approval of the Fuehrer ... Not I but the Fuehrer is the guardian of economic reason."
- (a) Frankfurter Zeitung, 19 Jan 1936: "In Germany there is fortunately only one policy and one economic policy, namely that of Adolf Hitler; to work with him and for his goals is the highest satisfaction for every member of the people's community."
- (a) Accepting the Golden Party Badge, 30 Jan 1937: "the German future lies in the hands of our Fuehrer." April 1937: "With the limitless passion of a burning heart and the infallible instinct of the born statesman, Adolf Hitler has won for himself the soul of the German people."
- (a) Vienna, 21 Mar 1938, to staff of the absorbed Austrian National Bank [EC-297-A]: "...it was only done by our Adolf Hitler ... A scoundrel he who breaks it [the oath]. To our Fuehrer a triple 'Sieg Heil'." The Tribunal noted he "has not contended that this speech did not represent his state of mind at the time."
- (a) Secret ministers' meeting, 12 May 1936 [1301-PS]: Schacht "stands with unswerving loyalty to the Fuehrer, because he fully recognizes the basic idea of National Socialism."
- (a) His retrospective, 30 Apr 1946: "It appears from this as if I had been a convinced adherent of Hitler at some time. I was never that." On Mein Kampf: "a book written in the worst kind of German, propaganda of ... a fanatical, half educated man, which to me Hitler has always been."
- (a) To Jackson, 2 May 1946: "I never deceived Hitler except after 1938." And: "I did not intend to defeat him. I intended to slow him down."

### 4. Jews
- (a) Konigsberg speech, 18 Aug 1935, two halves that must be quoted together. Prosecution excerpt [EC-433]: "The Jew must realize that their influence is gone for all times. We desire to keep our people and our culture pure and distinctive ... But the solution of these problems must be brought about under state leadership, and cannot be left to unregulated individual actions." Schacht's own excerpt: he attacked "people who under cover of darkness heroically smear window panes, who brand as a traitor every German who trades in a Jewish store"; and concluded: "the Jew can be neither a citizen nor a fellow German. But Point 5 of the Party program provides legislation for him too; that means, he must not be subjected to arbitrary action but to the law."
- (a) Letter to Blomberg, 24 Dec 1935 [EC-293]: the treatment of the Jews, anti-Church activity and Gestapo lawlessness "are detrimental to our armament program". His objection was framed in terms of rearmament and exports.
- (a) To Jackson, 2 May 1946: "I was always in favor of limiting Jewish participation in those fields, not actually according to the population, but nevertheless limiting them to a certain percentage."
- (a) Dec 1938: went to London for the Schacht-Rublee plan to finance Jewish emigration, "a thing which I myself had suggested", funded from expropriated Jewish assets. No contemporaneous public condemnation of Kristallnacht is in the trial record.

### 5. Rearmament, Mefo bills, the New Plan
- (a) Memorandum to Hitler, 3 May 1935 [1168-PS]: "the accomplishment of the armament program in regard to speed and extent, is the task of German policy, and that therefore everything else must be subordinated to this aim." And: "Our armaments are, therefore, being financed partially with the assets of our political opponents."
- (a) Speech, 29 Nov 1938 [EC-611]: "no bank of issue in peacetimes carried on such a daring credit policy as the Reichsbank since the seizure of power by National Socialism. With the aid of this credit policy, however, Germany created an armament second to none, and this armament in turn made possible the results of our policy." And: "There is no German financial miracle. There is only the miracle of the reawakening of German national consciousness and German discipline, and we owe this miracle to our Fuehrer Adolf Hitler."
- (a) Letter to Goring, 5 Aug 1937: "The aim and the idea of the Four Year Plan were and remain entirely correct and necessary!"
- (a) Nuremberg, 1 May 1946: "Mefo bills, of course, were a thoroughly risky operation, but they were absolutely not risky if they were connected with a reasonable financial procedure ... But Herr Hitler preferred simply to refuse to pay the bills back." Reichsbank credit to March 1938: 12 billion RM, "about one third of the amount which was spent on rearmament."
- (a) 2 May 1946: "I am still satisfied today that I contributed to the rearmament, but I wish that Hitler had made different use of it."

### 6. Central bank cooperation, Norman, the BIS
- (a) Schacht coined the name "International Settlements Bank" in a Feb 1929 memo to Owen Young and pushed for the BIS to be a long-term development lender. France and the commercial banks blocked it. [Toniolo 2005 ch. 2; Ahamed] The verbatim memo text was not retrieved.
- (a) The Magic of Money: "Whereas before the world wars the gold standard regulated international payments without any difficulty, today there are a whole series of organisations like the World Bank ... the International Monetary Fund and others, which seek to adjust the value of the various currencies to each other."
- (a) BoE Governor's file G1/415 "Dr Hjalmar Schacht" runs 27 Mar 1933 to 23 Mar 1953: the correspondence outlasted the war and Nuremberg.
- (c) No Schacht source exists for the BIS being designed to "rule the world" or form a "world government of bankers". That gloss is Carroll Quigley's.

### 7. Religion, Freemasonry, democracy
- (a) 30 Apr 1946: "My father, throughout his life, adhered to democratic ideals. He was a Freemason. He was a cosmopolitan ... I have never departed from these basic conceptions of Freemasonry and democracy and humanitarian and cosmopolitan ideals." He joined the lodge Urania zur Unsterblichkeit in 1908 and co-founded the liberal DDP in 1919.
- (a) "A Weltanschauung which is not rooted in religion is in my opinion no Weltanschauung at all. Consequently I reject the National Socialist Weltanschauung which was not rooted in religion."
- (a) "As a convinced adherent to the Christian faith I believe in Christian charity, which bids me extend love to all men without regard to race or faith." Same session, on the Nazi leaders: "excessive drinking was a main part of the Nazi ideology."
- (a) Why Hitler had to be Chancellor: "to me, as a democrat and a follower of democratic parliamentary government, it was quite inevitable that that man was now to be entrusted with forming a cabinet ... There was only one other possibility ... a military rule."
- (a) Epigraph to 76 Jahre meines Lebens (1953): "Neither force nor money shape the world; spiritual strength and moral action can transform worlds."
- (a) Opening of Confessions of the Old Wizard (1956): "Prison air is the same the world over. I had been imprisoned for conspiring against Hitler. After Hitler was dead, I was imprisoned for aiding him."
- (b) To prison psychiatrist Goldensohn, 1946: "There was only the choice between Communism and Hitler." "I have never believed in war. It is a crime against humanity whether you win or lose." "I detest uniforms because they make one unfree."
- (a) On why he never became a martyr, 2 May 1946: "Martyrs can be effective only if their martyrdom becomes known to the public."
- (a) On his self-image: "I said 'No' to all those things. I blocked credits. I opposed an excessive rearmament ... I do not know to whom else this honorary title of 'No man' might apply if not to me."
- IMT Judgment: "Schacht was a central figure in Germany's rearmament programme ... But rearmament of itself is not criminal under the Charter ... this necessary inference [that he knew of aggressive plans] has not been established beyond a reasonable doubt." Acquitted.
- Contemporaries: Robert Jackson: "the most dangerous and reprehensible type of all opportunists, someone who would use a Hitler for his own ends, and then claim, after Hitler was defeated, to have been against him all the time." George Messersmith: "by Schacht's resourcefulness, his complete financial ruthlessness, and his absolute cynicism, Schacht was able to maintain and to establish the situation for the Nazis." Paul Schmidt: "Schacht has intelligence, but is not particularly a man of principle, although I think he believes he is." Hitler's dismissal letter, 19 Jan 1939, added in his own hand: "Your name, above all, will always be connected with the first epoch of the national rearmament."

## PART III. Where the two creeds met

1. Both held that central banks should stand above governments. Norman wrote it into his 1921 manifesto and tried to write it into the BIS statutes. Schacht's 7 Jan 1939 memo is the same doctrine turned against Hitler, and it got him fired.
2. Both treated Versailles reparations as an economic absurdity. Norman: Germany "needs help & comfort rather than worrying". Schacht: "I fought against this nonsense."
3. Both saw the BIS as their instrument. Norman wanted "an umbrella" and a bank "beyond the reach of governments". Schacht wanted a development lender for Germany. Neither got quite what he wanted.
4. The friendship was personal and durable: Norman travelled to Berlin in January 1939 to stand godfather to Schacht's grandson, kept a correspondence file on him until 1953, and told the US Treasury that Schacht was his main window on Germany for sixteen years.
5. Where they diverged: Norman's creed was genuinely internationalist and anti-political. Schacht's was nationalist first; independence of the Reichsbank was a tool for German power, and when Hitler offered faster rearmament than sound money allowed, Schacht financed it for five years before objecting.

## PART IV. Quotes to avoid
- Norman "1924" hegemony of world finance quote: fabricated.
- Norman "currency dictator of Europe" admission of 21 March 1930: only in Quigley, unsourced.
- Norman "knave-proof": Bradbury's word.
- Norman "I have instincts": apocryphal.
- Norman "Hitler and Schacht are the bulwarks of civilisation": hearsay at best.
- Schacht "the BIS will rule the world": Quigley's gloss, not Schacht.
- Schacht "I have never been a Nazi": paraphrase; verbatim forms are "I have never been a member of the Party" and "I was never that".
- Schacht Konigsberg 1935 quoted only as anti-violence: half the speech.
- "The first cause of stability of our currency is the concentration camp": Hitler, not Schacht.
