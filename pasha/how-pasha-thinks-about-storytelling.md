# How Kamran Pasha Thinks About Writing and Storytelling

Compiled from his Patreon emails in garrethdottin@gmail.com: "The Pasha Method of Storytelling" (written Sept 2021, reposted about 15 times between 2023 and 2026), Q&A #12–#18 (2023–2025), and two posts that mention the method. The source texts are in this folder. Quotes are kept short; everything else is paraphrased.

---

## 1. The core belief: structure comes first

- He puts his whole career down to one thing: **"STRUCTURE is the key to my writing."** A producer once called his first script (on the Crusades) the best-structured script they had read in twenty years, and he treats that as his calling card.
- To him, structure is where story and character come from. Good structure lets the story unfold naturally and lets the character grow. Bad structure gives you "confused storytelling and flat characters." His go-to example of bad structure is *The Last Jedi*.
- He believes structure should hold you back from wandering. He says the method "forces you to come up with a story that works the first time" and that **"structure compels story and keeps you from deviating based on your whims"** (Q&A #17).
- Speed follows from structure. He says he wrote the Crusades script in 5 days, *Taj Mahal* in 4 (sold to Warner Bros), an Emmy-submitted *Sleeper Cell* episode in one 15-hour session, and the *Andalus* pilot in 10 days (sold to Universal). He says the method lets him outline any script "within one hour."

## 2. The foundation: Aristotle's three acts

He teaches the three acts first, because the method depends on them. His page numbers are for a 120-page screenplay:

| Section | Pages | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Act One** | 1–30 | Meet the Hero, especially his *flaws and weaknesses*. His goal is set up, and so is the Villain who blocks it. The act ends when the Hero is pushed into the journey. |
| **Act Two, first half** | 30–60 | The main adventure builds toward the... |
| **MIDPOINT** | ~60 | The major turning point of the whole story. It's the point of no return, often the Hero's *first real victory*, and the moment he starts becoming the Hero. |
| **Act Two, second half** | 60–90/100 | New challenges pile on pressure until the Hero breaks. |
| **End of Act Two** | ~90–100 | The Hero's *worst possible moment*. He's suffered a major loss and failure looks certain. |
| **Act Three** | 90/100–120 | He pulls himself up, learns the lesson he needed, defeats the Villain and reaches his goal. |

- He explains why stories have three parts with the Riddle of the Sphinx: morning, noon and evening make up a man's life. Beginning, middle and end is how people see their own lives, so it's how they take in stories.
- Books he recommends: Aristotle's *Poetics*, Christopher Vogler's *The Writer's Journey* ("one of the most important books on story ever written"), and, for first novels, David Morrell's *The Successful Novelist* (Q&A #14).

## 3. The Pasha Method: Beginning, End, then Middle

His own summary, in the bolded paragraph of the article: work out where the story **starts** and where it **ends**, use those two points to find the **logical midpoint**, then *repeat the process between each pair of neighbouring points* until the whole script is filled in.

### The order you work in
1. **Beginning.** Who is the Hero at the start? (Luke on the farm, dreaming of adventure, but his uncle won't let him leave.)
2. **End.** Who is he at the finish? (Luke defeats the Empire and gets a medal from the Princess.)
3. **Midpoint.** What has to happen halfway between those two versions of him? The answer is the *first time he acts as the Hero*. (Luke rescues the Princess.)
4. **End of Act One**, halfway between the Beginning and the Midpoint: the Hero sets off on the journey. The story *needs* whatever pushes him out. His uncle won't let him go, so the story has to kill the uncle and aunt.
5. **End of Act Two**, halfway between the Midpoint and the End: things go terribly wrong. (Ben dies, and the Death Star tracks them to the Rebel base.)
6. **Quarter-points**, the middle of each gap:
   - Beginning → End of Act One: *he has to learn about the Princess* (the hologram).
   - End of Act One → Midpoint: *he has to get onto the Death Star* (the ship is captured).
   - Midpoint → End of Act Two: *the escape has to go wrong* (the trash compactor).
7. **Keep going down to single scenes.**
   - Hologram → leaving with Ben: *he has to meet Ben* (Ben saves him from the Sand People, which shows Luke isn't a hero yet).
   - Leaving → Death Star: *he needs a way off the planet* (Han Solo).

### The finished Star Wars outline (his version)
| Point | Beat |
|---|---|
| Beginning | Luke on the farm, dreaming of adventure; his uncle won't let him leave |
| Mid Act One | Luke finds the Princess's hologram asking for help |
| End Act One | Uncle and aunt killed; Luke leaves with Ben to rescue the Princess |
| Mid first half of Act Two | Luke arrives on the Death Star |
| **Midpoint** | **Luke rescues the Princess** |
| Mid second half of Act Two | Luke trapped in the trash compactor while escaping |
| End Act Two | Ben killed; the Death Star tracks them to the Rebel base |
| End | Luke destroys the Death Star; medal from the Princess |

### The same method on his own Crusades script
- **Beginning:** Saladin conquers Jerusalem and defeats the Crusaders.
- **End:** Saladin and Richard shake hands on a truce over Jerusalem.
- **Midpoint:** Richard beats Saladin at the siege of Acre, undoing the sultan's first victory.
- **End of Act One:** Richard arrives in the Holy Land.
- **End of Act Two:** Richard moves on Jerusalem, Saladin's worst moment.
- **Mid Act One:** Richard hears Jerusalem has fallen and sets out on the Third Crusade.

The pattern: the Midpoint is often the *reverse* of the Beginning (Saladin wins, then Saladin loses). The End then *resolves* both.

### What I notice about it (my reading, not his words)
- It's **repeated halving**. Each new beat splits an existing gap in two, so the beats fall on pages 60, then 30 and 90, then 15, 45, 75 and 105. That lines up exactly with the standard act breaks, so the act structure comes out of the method automatically.
- Each question he asks is about **causes**: *what has to happen* for the character to get from A to B? Scenes come from what the character needs, not from spectacle.
- The questions are always about **who the Hero is**, not only what happens. He puts it this way: who the Hero is at the Beginning and the End is decided by who he is at the Midpoint.

## 4. Why the midpoint matters so much: the "liminal phase"

- He studied Religion in college, and he roots the method in scripture. Great religious stories last because they match how people experience time: we always feel we're *in the middle*, between our past and our future.
- Religious-studies scholars call that in-between stage the **liminal phase**: the moment the hero faces his past and his future and has to choose.
- His three examples:
  - **Moses.** Prince at the start, prophet at the end. The midpoint is the exiled shepherd at the **Burning Bush**, who is neither prince nor prophet, and is given his mission.
  - **Jesus.** Carpenter at the start, Messiah at the end. The midpoint is **40 days in the wilderness**, tempted by Satan. Refusing the temptation is the choice that starts his mission.
  - **Muhammad.** Rejected in Mecca at the start, ruler of Arabia at the end. The midpoint is the **Hijrah**, so central that the Islamic calendar starts from it.
- He uses the same test on current shows. He praised *Ahsoka* Episode 4, the midpoint of its 8-episode season, for going "into the Liminal World (literally)," and joked that Filoni had been reading the Pasha Method.
- He ends the article by applying it to the reader: *you're at the Midpoint of your own journey*, and your choices now shape the rest of your story.

## 5. Craft principles from the Q&As

**Plot vs. character.** He prefers **plot-driven** stories, because "true character is revealed through conflict and crisis." He finds character-driven work can turn "too artsy and self-absorbed" (Q&A #15).

**Themes.** He says he doesn't think in themes. He writes the stories that draw him in, and describes his work as **"epic and intimate"**: big historical events seen through people caught up in forces they can't control (Q&A #17).

**Dialogue and distinct voices** (Q&A #16):
- If every character sounds the same, *you haven't done the character work*. You don't yet see each person as unique.
- Fixes:
  - Read the dialogue out loud; your ear catches repetition.
  - Write a backstory for each character that explains how they see the world.
  - Work out how someone with that worldview would talk.
  - Study Tarantino, and Paul Thomas Anderson (though he says PTA gives up act structure to let characters lead).
- His negative example is Kostova's *The Historian*: great research and setting, but every character had the same rhythm of speech.

**Character arcs need setup.** He'd fix the final season of *Game of Thrones* by *building* to Daenerys's turn over several seasons, so the audience roots for her to resist her dark side and is heartbroken when she gives in, the way it works with Anakin. A sudden unearned change is a structural failure (Q&A #14).

**World-building.** A world can grow forever *as long as its rules stay consistent*. Roddenberry and Lucas are his masters of this. Breaking a world's own rules is how you spot someone ruining a franchise. Throwing things at the wall (his example is Lindelof's *Lost*) is "incompetence masked as creative freedom" (Q&A #12).

**Adapting someone else's work.** Find the core of what made the original work and honour it, even as you take it somewhere new (Q&A #15).

**Homage.** It's fine when it *honours* the past, as in *Ghostbusters: Afterlife*. It's contempt when it mocks the past, as with the Bond lines in *No Time to Die*. "There can be no present without acknowledging the past" (Q&A #12).

**Rewrites.** "All professional writing is rewriting." When studio notes change his ending, he runs the method again to rebalance the beats. His point: if you know the beginning and the end, *the middle can always be made to work* (Q&A #13).

**Scripts are never finished.** He quotes da Vinci that art is only abandoned. Productions often shoot before the script is ready, and rewrites happen on set (Q&A #14).

**Small craft notes.** Use ellipses "sparingly…" (Q&A #16). You can't copyright an idea, only the actual words (Q&A #17).

## 6. How he sees the creative life

- **Writing is compulsion, not choice.** He says nobody sane would choose it. The Muse "takes the writer hostage," and art brings beauty to humanity even if it destroys the artist. His examples are van Gogh and Marilyn Monroe (Q&A #15).
- **Pressure makes art.** Financial, emotional and psychological strain turns coal into a diamond. He thinks financial security is "the death knell of creation," and that few masterpieces come from comfort (Q&A #18).
- **Artists vs. patrons.** Executives are like the Medicis: they fund genius but resent it, so they push their own "creativity" into the work and make it worse. He sees contempt for writers as coming from insecurity. "Everybody believes they can write" (Q&A #17, #18).
- **Art comes from consciousness, not machines.** AI can mimic but "cannot create something new." He draws on David Bohm's idea that consciousness is fundamental and creativity comes from that field (Q&A #12, #15).
- **Timing.** A lasting hit needs to meet the zeitgeist. He says many of his scripts were ahead of their time (*Taj Mahal* before *Slumdog Millionaire*, *Andalus*, a *Tigana* pitch turned down weeks before HBO announced *Game of Thrones*). He compares himself to Cassandra (Q&A #12, #13, #17).
- **Archetypes follow the times.** Tropes spread "like a virus" when they fit the national mood. His examples: the Labyrinth in the trapped, Watergate-era 1970s (*Star Wars*, *The Shining*, *Alien*), and villains who *want* to be caught after the Madoff era (Q&A #13).
- **The best writers room** was Ira Steven Behr's on *The Twilight Zone*: everyone not writing a script sat in the room pitching all the time. He runs his own rooms that way. His bad examples are *Bionic Woman* (no room at all), *Nikita* (two rooms split by who the showrunner liked) and *Kings* (a showrunner who secretly kept his first pitch) (Q&A #13).
- **Advice he'd give his younger self:** "SAVE MONEY!" (Q&A #14).

## 7. Things to keep in mind when reading him

- He opens the method with a long account of being shut out by "The Club," a Hollywood establishment he says blocked a Muslim writer from Pakistan. He presents his speed and structural skill as the reason he survived anyway. The craft advice stands on its own, but it's wrapped in that grievance and self-promotion (he compares himself favourably to Kelley and Sorkin).
- The Q&As mix craft with strong opinions about politics and industry figures (Kennedy, Iger, "Woke," the WGA). I've left those out except where they show how he judges stories.
- The method is part-teaching, part-sales pitch. It ends with an offer of paid consulting, and one guest post is a client testimonial ("The Pasha Method works!" from Andrew Smith, author of *Our Lady of the Artilects*).
